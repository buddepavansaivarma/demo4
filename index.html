Artifact 01 — Required Information

Introduction

Choosing a machine learning model begins with understanding what it learns, the data it uses, and the problem it is meant to solve. This visual framework translates that selection process into an accessible guide for fellow students, instructors, and professionals exploring applied AI.

Description

The primary artifact is a high-resolution infographic comparing ten methods: logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, convolutional neural networks, K-means, principal component analysis, BERT encoders, autoregressive Transformers, generative adversarial networks, and diffusion models. The framework distinguishes learning objectives, identifies applicable domains, and illustrates each method with a practical use case. This supporting report expands the classifications and describes the relationships among methods.

Objective

To accurately identify and classify at least ten AI/ML methods, communicate the distinctions between supervised, unsupervised, and self-supervised objectives, connect the methods to tabular data, computer vision, NLP, and generative AI, and demonstrate systematic model-selection reasoning.

Process

I selected representative algorithms spanning conventional predictive modeling, structure discovery, foundation models, and generative AI. I then classified their usual training objectives based on the source of learning targets, separately tagged relevant application domains, and wrote concise descriptions and examples. I mapped relationships such as decision trees to random forests, PCA to K-means, and BERT to generative Transformers. Finally, I organized the methods in a visual layout and checked both the classifications and supporting explanations against technical resources and the assignment rubric.

Tools and Technologies Used

ChatGPT for drafting and organization; Python and python-docx for preparing the portfolio materials; Microsoft Word-compatible document formatting; PDF and PNG exports for accessible sharing; and technical documentation and research papers for algorithm verification.

Value Proposition

The artifact makes algorithm selection easier to explain to mixed technical and nontechnical audiences. It demonstrates the ability to communicate complex machine learning concepts, choose suitable approaches for a business problem, and distinguish prediction, representation learning, and content generation.

Unique Value

Rather than grouping every method by a single label, the framework uses two complementary dimensions: the training signal and the application domain. It also flags that BERT and generative Transformers generally use self-supervised pretraining and that CNN and GAN variants may use different objectives. Those nuances make the guide more useful than a simple supervised-versus-unsupervised list.

Relevance

This work is relevant to data science, machine learning engineering, and AI solution design. The included utility-outage example illustrates how structured records, field images, text notes, and generative reporting can be combined in a responsible workflow. It provides reusable evidence of technical understanding and professional communication.

What the six components do

01. Layers

Organize neurons into input, hidden, and output stages that transform features into predictions.

02. Neurons

Compute weighted sums plus bias, then pass the result through an activation function.

03. Weights

Learnable parameters that control how strongly each input contributes to a neuron's calculation.

04. Activation Functions

Introduce nonlinearity, allowing multiple layers to model complex relationships rather than only linear ones.

05. Loss Functions

Quantify disagreement between model predictions and target values; they provide a training objective.

06. Optimization Algorithms

Use gradients to update weights and biases, commonly through stochastic gradient descent or Adam.

Artifact 02 — Project Description & Learning Process

Introduction

Neural networks learn mappings between inputs and desired outputs by repeatedly applying weighted calculations and nonlinear transformations. Visualizing their architecture makes the connection between a prediction and the learning mechanisms easier to understand.

Objective

Explain six fundamental components, map the information flow through a network, and clarify how prediction errors guide weight updates. Demonstrate conceptual understanding using clear visual communication.

Classification Rationale

I grouped elements by their primary role: structure (layers and neurons), computation (weights and activation functions), and learning (loss functions and optimizers). The grouping separates where computation occurs, how outputs are formed, and how parameters improve.

Process

I mapped a feedforward path from inputs through hidden layers to an output, then distinguished that forward pass from the training feedback loop. I compared ReLU, sigmoid, and tanh functions visually and designed controlled Playground comparisons to isolate one parameter at a time.

Tools & Skills

Neural-network diagrams, mathematical reasoning, activation-function visualization, PowerPoint/PDF communication, experimental design, and TensorFlow Playground configuration. These support machine learning and AI education.

Value & Relevance

Unlike Artifact 01, which categorizes algorithm families, this artifact explains internal model behavior. It offers a practical reference for communicating neural-network design, learning trade-offs, and oversight to technical and nontechnical audiences.

Artifact 03 — Project Description & Design Rationale

Introduction

Training advanced large language models requires far more than a single training run. It involves collecting and preparing large datasets, pretraining on massive compute clusters, aligning the model through post-training, and validating performance and safety before deployment. Because these stages consume substantial resources, understanding the full training pipeline is important for both technical and business audiences.

Description

The artifact is a portfolio infographic that visualizes six major stages of LLM development: data collection and licensing, data cleaning and tokenization, pretraining, post-training and alignment, evaluation and red-teaming, and deployment with continuous monitoring. It then classifies the main cost drivers into four categories—data, compute, energy, and time/people—and closes with a comparison of publicly available information for GPT-3, Llama 3, Gemini 1.5, and Claude.

Objective

To communicate the hidden infrastructure behind generative AI in a way that is technically accurate, visually clear, and understandable to varied audiences. The project aims to show how model quality depends not only on algorithm design, but also on data acquisition, computational scale, safety tuning, and the transparency of providers.

Classification Process

I classified the content in two layers. First, I organized the training workflow as a pipeline, showing how each stage contributes to the final model. Second, I grouped resource expenditures into four categories: data, compute, energy, and time/people. This structure makes it easier to see both when costs are incurred and what type of resource each stage depends on. I then separated model examples into two groups: models with notable publicly disclosed training details and models whose exact costs remain undisclosed.

Process

I reviewed official documentation and technical reports related to OpenAI, Meta, Google, and Anthropic. I extracted only the details that were publicly disclosed, such as GPT-3 training scale and Llama 3 GPU-hour and emissions figures. When information was absent in official model documentation, I represented that absence clearly in the design instead of filling it with unsupported estimates. I then converted the research into a visual hierarchy with a process flow, classification panels, and concise model snapshots.

Tools & Technologies Used

ChatGPT for research support and writing assistance, official technical reports and model cards, image-generation support for infographic design, Microsoft Word-compatible documentation, and HTML/CSS portfolio integration. Core competencies demonstrated include AI communication, information classification, research synthesis, and visual design.

Value Proposition

This artifact demonstrates my ability to interpret technical AI infrastructure topics and explain them clearly through professional visual communication. It is useful for product stakeholders, students, or non-specialists who need to understand why frontier AI systems are costly to build and why public transparency around those costs matters.

Relevance & Learning Reflection

The project deepened my understanding that generative AI performance is inseparable from data pipelines, computational scale, and safety processes. A key insight was that the most powerful models are also the least transparent in some cost dimensions. That realization strengthened my appreciation for responsible communication: an accurate artifact should explain both what is known and what remains undisclosed. This is directly relevant to careers in machine learning, data science, and AI product strategy, where technical judgment and clear stakeholder communication are equally important.
